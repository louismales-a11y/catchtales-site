Hook, Line & Sinkers — The Complete Terminal Tackle Guide

A complete guide to terminal tackle: hooks, fishing lines, and sinkers. Learn how to choose the right setup for any fishing situation.

Terminal tackle is everything at the end of your main fishing line — hooks, leaders, swivels, snaps, and sinkers. Getting these right is the difference between a great day on the water and a frustrating one. This guide covers the full range of terminal tackle so you can match your gear to the fish you're targeting.

Hook Styles

There are over 15 hook styles, each designed for specific bait, techniques, and fish species. Here's a breakdown of the most common ones.

J-Hooks & Circle Hooks

J-hooks are the universal standard — a simple J-shape with the point parallel to the shank. They work for almost every fishing situation. Circle hooks curve inward so the point faces the shank, which reduces gut-hooking and makes them ideal for catch-and-release fishing. When a fish takes the bait, circle hooks slide to the corner of the mouth and set themselves.

Octopus & Kahle Hooks

Octopus hooks have a short shank and are excellent for live bait — the short shank lets the bait swim more naturally. Kahle hooks have a distinctive wide bend and are commonly used in current fishing, where the hook design helps hook fish as they sweep past.

Aberdeen & Baitholder Hooks

Aberdeen hooks have a long shank and light wire, making them perfect for panfish and small bait. The long shank makes hook removal easy. Baitholder hooks have barbs on the shank that hold worms and other soft baits in place — a favorite for worm fishing.

Wide Gap & Offset Hooks

Designed for soft plastic baits. Wide gap hooks leave room between the hook point and shank for bulky plastics, ensuring solid hooksets. Offset hooks have the point bent slightly away from the shank, increasing hook-up ratios with plastic worms, creature baits, and swimbaits.

Treble, Siwash & Weedless

Treble hooks have three points on a single shank and are standard on most hard lures like crankbaits and spoons. Siwash hooks replace trebles on lures where single hooks are preferred or required. Weedless hooks have a wire guard that deflects weeds and snags, essential for fishing heavy cover.

Egg Hooks & Specialty Designs

Egg hooks have a short, wide-gap design for baiting with salmon or trout eggs. Specialty designs include Snell hooks (pre-tied for strength), Demon hooks (multiple points for bait presentation), and Double hooks (two points for rigging specific baits).

Hook Sizing & Wire Gauges

Hook sizes are numbered inversely — a size 32 hook is tiny (for the smallest panfish and trout), while a size 20/0 hook is massive (for big game tuna and marlin). The "aught" scale (1/0, 2/0, etc.) starts where the regular scale ends — size 1 is the largest on the regular scale, then 1/0 continues upward.

Wire gauges determine hook strength:

  • Fine wire — Light, penetrates easily, good for small baits and panfish
  • Medium wire — The standard all-purpose gauge
  • Heavy wire — Stronger, for bigger fish and tougher conditions
  • Extra heavy wire — For big game saltwater fishing and the largest freshwater species

Hook Finishes & Coatings

Hook finishes affect visibility, corrosion resistance, and penetration:

  • Bronze/Norway — Standard finish, decent corrosion resistance
  • Nickel — Bright finish, more visible to fish
  • Chrome — Reflective, good for flash-attracting lures
  • Gold — Popular for salmon and steelhead, less visible underwater
  • Black Nickel — Nearly invisible under water, good for clear conditions
  • Tinned — Saltwater-grade corrosion protection
  • Chemically Sharpened — Etched point for razor-sharp penetration

Fishing Line Types

There are six main categories of fishing line, each with unique properties:

Monofilament

The classic all-rounder. Mono stretches under pressure (good shock absorber), floats on water, and is the most affordable option. Great for beginners, topwater lures, and general freshwater fishing. Available from 2 lb test to over 100 lb.

Fluorocarbon

Nearly invisible underwater — fluorocarbon has a similar refractive index to water. It sinks, is more abrasion-resistant than mono, and has less stretch. Ideal for leader material and fishing in clear water where fish are line-shy.

Braid

Zero-stretch powerhouse. Braid is incredibly thin for its strength — 30 lb braid has the same diameter as 8 lb mono. No stretch means instant hooksets at distance. Excellent for heavy cover, deep water, and long casts. Must use a leader in clear water.

Copolymer & Fused Lines

Copolymer blends two or more polymers to combine the best properties of mono and fluoro — less stretch, more abrasion resistance, and better knot strength. Fused lines like Fireline are heat-fused gel-spun polyethylene — stronger than mono but with more stretch than braid.

Specialty Lines

Fly line comes in floating, intermediate, and sinking varieties for fly fishing. Dacron is a braided polyester used for backing. Lead core line has a lead strand for trolling at precise depths. Wire line is used for toothy fish like pike and muskie (as a leader) or deep trolling.

Line Selection by Situation

Choose your line based on conditions:

  • Clear water: Fluorocarbon leader (invisible)
  • Topwater: Monofilament (floats)
  • Heavy cover: Braid (zero stretch, powerful hooksets)
  • Deep water: Braid with fluoro leader
  • Beginners: Monofilament (forgiving, cheap)
  • Leader material: Fluorocarbon (abrasion-resistant)

Sinker Styles

Weight styles are designed for specific bottom conditions and presentations:

  • Egg & Bullet — Sliding weights for Texas rigs and Carolina rigs. Egg-shaped for snag-free bottom fishing, bullet-shaped for weedless presentations.
  • Drop Shot & Mojo — Drop shot weights are long and thin, suspending the bait off the bottom. Mojo weights are cylindrical and pinch onto the line.
  • Bank & Pyramid — Bank sinkers cast well in rivers and lakes. Pyramid sinkers have a square shape that digs into sand, perfect for surf casting.
  • Walking, No-Roll & Bell — Walking sinkers slide along the bottom without snagging. No-roll sinkers stay put on slopes. Bell sinkers have a wide base for still fishing in current.
  • Rubber Core & Clip-On — Quick-change weights that attach with a rubber core or spring clip. No retying needed to change weight.
  • Dipsey — Trolling weight with a fin that planes downward, taking your bait deep and keeping it there at slower speeds.
  • Bottom Bouncers — A weighted wire arm that keeps your bait near the bottom while the weight bounces along. Designed for walleye trolling in rivers.

Putting It All Together

Building effective rigs starts with choosing the right terminal tackle for your target species and conditions. Common rigs include:

  • Bottom rig / Running rig — Egg sinker sliding above a swivel, with a leader and hook below. Simple, effective for bottom-feeding fish.
  • Texas rig — Bullet weight pegged above a wide-gap hook with a soft plastic. Weedless and versatile.
  • Drop shot rig — Weight at the very end of the line with a hook tied above using a Palomar knot. Keeps the bait suspended off the bottom.
  • Carolina rig — Egg sinker on the main line, followed by a bead, swivel, then a long leader with a hook. Good for dragging baits along the bottom.
